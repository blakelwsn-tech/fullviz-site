About

I can’t walk past something broken.

Twelve-plus years in retail, from the sales floor to the software. This is the short version.

I grew up on a small family farm in southern Idaho. I raised pigs and did FFA in high school. I was supposed to become a cowboy, but I hung up my spurs for a baseball bat. (I still get back to my favorite place in the world, my grandparents’ cattle ranch in northern Nevada, which belongs to the extended family now.)

I studied Kinesiology at Biola, which is the science of how bodies move. Then I took a summer internship at Target my junior year and found out I was more interested in how a store moves. The internship turned into a job offer, and I haven’t left retail since.

Most of what came next was at big companies, but nearly always in the part that was still a startup. I’ve done 0 to 1 enough times to know when to be scrappy, and to recognize the signals that it’s time to scale and build for durability. Those are the stages I like to hang out in. (What the stages mean.)

Target

Brick-and-mortar retail

My first real job was running asset protection at a Target in Los Angeles that did about $65 million a year. Asset protection is a formal way of saying I was responsible for everything that went missing. We cut shrink 15% in a year. Then I moved to store operations as second in command to the store director, with more than 300 people on the team at peak and 20,000 payroll hours a month to answer for. For a stretch I was the interim store director, in one of LA’s highest-volume stores.

Stores and warehouses teach you something that software people learn late. A process only exists if the person on the floor can run it.

Amazon · 0 to 1, then 1 to 2

Digital retail at the everything store

In 2015 I joined Amazon’s Prime Now. I trained at and helped launch its fourth US site, an early one in the Midwest, then launched the seventh, my home site in Orange County, California. There were 24 by the time I moved on, and I’d helped launch eight. Nobody had written the manual for a warehouse that delivers in an hour or two, so we wrote it as we went. I built delivery routes by hand until engineering turned the logic into a product.

Then I got into the data, and I never really got out. I owned inventory planning, first for the Southwest and then for hardlines nationwide, and I built the planning tool in SQL and Excel because the one I needed didn’t exist. I helped launch Amazon’s first four multi-temperature grocery hubs, and worked the assortment and inventory side of relaunching more than 45 urban fulfillment centers. Each one was a full flip: new shelving, new assortment, new website, new back end. Without the hubs, those sites wouldn’t have had the grocery and CPG assortment they needed.

I finished at Prime Wardrobe, the try-before-you-buy program. It had found its market, and now it had to make money. That’s the 1-to-2 stage. About two of every three items came back, and my job was to make that a business anyway.

Six years, four jobs. I learned that I like the seam between operations and product more than either side of it. That gap is where I can be a multiplier across teams.

SAP · 0 to 1

The software company

I started FullViz in late 2021. A few months later SAP hired me to build a retail store product line from scratch. There was no roadmap, so the first job was to write one. I led nine product managers across the US, Germany, and China, and went to Denmark, Germany, and Israel to sit with retailers and find out what they needed. I’d spent nearly a decade on the receiving end of enterprise software. Now I got to see how it’s made (it explained a lot).

The roadmap won a $10 million engineering investment. We launched Intelligent Returns, the first product the line shipped, and cut the share of engineering time going to technical debt from over 30% to under 10%. FullViz kept going alongside it.

Nuna Baby, Gorjana

The brands

After SAP I went in-house at two consumer brands. At Nuna Baby I worked from purchase order to sale across four brands and nine countries, and set up the first business review that put all of them on the same page with the same numbers.

At Gorjana I ran supply chain through a warehouse move and a holiday peak, with 123 stores to keep stocked. I owned the operations side of opening new stores (eight of them while I was there), and I automated the returns flow.

Now

FullViz

FullViz is where all of that lands. Small and growing retail and consumer brands hire me when the business has outgrown the way it runs. I work on the systems and the operations at the same time, because I’ve never seen one get fixed without the other.

Patterns

Two things that kept showing up

That was the résumé. These ran through all of it.

Pattern 01

Returns keep finding me

Three times now, I’ve ended up owning what happens when the product comes back. At Prime Wardrobe it was return performance and cost. At SAP it was Intelligent Returns, as a product. At Gorjana it was the returns flow in the warehouse and the customer care behind it.

Every return has a sunk cost, and a company needs a way to manage it. Most also undervalue what a good return does for loyalty. The returns notes are here.

Pattern 02

I’ll take the trip

I’ll gladly use business as a reason to travel, and I like working with people around the world. Early in Prime Now I went to the UK to help turn a site around. At Prime Wardrobe we launched in France, Italy, and Spain. At SAP my team sat in Germany, China, and the US, and I met retailers in Denmark, Germany, and Israel. At Nuna the numbers came from nine countries.

Retail is a sum of its parts. Shoppers behave differently from one country to the next. The operational needs and the technical tenets underneath stay the same.

The name

Why a lighthouse

A lighthouse gives direction in darkness and in storms, when there isn’t a clear path forward. It can’t steer the ship for you. But it shows you where the rocks are, and most businesses I meet don’t know how much they can’t see until they hit something.

That’s the job, and it’s the name. Full visibility.

A short manual

Working with me

  • I’m direct. If the process is the problem, I’ll say so, and then I’ll help fix it.
  • You can’t make a decision about something you don’t understand. But you’ll never have the perfect amount of data, either. So I get comfortable deciding with what’s in front of me, and I keep moving toward more clarity over time.
  • An anecdote matters as much as a metric. Some problems never show up in the numbers, and you have to go find the root cause yourself. (When a measure becomes the target, it stops being a good measure. That’s Goodhart’s law. I always forget the name.)
  • I’m quick on small decisions and slow on big ones. That’s Jeff Bezos’s one-way and two-way doors, which I picked up at Amazon. If you can walk back through the door, decide now. If you can’t, slow down.
  • I over-build. Left alone, I’ll design the complete version of a thing when the rough one would do. So tell me which one you need.
  • I care about the people who have to run it after I leave. A system nobody wants to use is a broken system.
